% Abstract section.
\begin{abstract}
    This document derives the charging behavior of a resistor-capacitor (RC) network, the voltage reached after one time constant, and applies the result to size a power-on-reset (POR) circuit for a microcontroller with an active-low reset.
\end{abstract}

% Introduction section.
\section{Introduction}
When a capacitor \textit{C} is charged through a series resistor \textit{R} from a constant supply \textit{V$_0$}, the capacitor voltage rises according to\\
\begin{equation}
    V(t)=V_0\left(1-e^{-t/RC}\right),
    \label{Equation1}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\tau=\textit{RC}$ is the time constant.

% Second section (Voltage at the Time Constant).
\section{Voltage at the Time Constant}
\noindent Substituting $t=\tau$ into (\ref{Equation1}):

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        &V(t)=V_0\left(1-e^{-\tau/RC}\right)\\
        &=V_0\left(1-e^{-RC/RC}\right)
        && \text{since } \tau=RC\\
        &=V_0\left(1-e^{-1}\right)\\
        &\approx V_0\left(1-0.368\right)\\
        &\approx 0.632V_0.\\
    \end{aligned}
    \label{Equation2}
\end{equation}

The exponent cancelling to -1 in (\ref{Equation2}) is why the 0.632 fraction is universal --- \textit{R} and \textit{C} dropped out of the calculation before the final line, so this fraction holds for any \textit{RC} network.  Substituting  $V_0=5.0V$:

\begin{equation}
    V(\tau)\approx0.632\times5.0V\approx 3.16V
\end{equation}
matching the dashed line in Figure~\ref{fig:rc}.

\section{Power on Reset With an Active Low Reset}
A power-on-reset circuit exploits (\ref{Equation1}) directly, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:mcu}: \textit{R} from $V_{CC}$ to the reset node and \textit{C} from that node to ground hold the microcontroller's active-low reset pin low until the node crosses $V_{IH}$, releasing the device.

% Subsection A of the third section (Sizing R for a Timing Constraint).
\subsection{Sizing R for a Timing Constraint}
The reset pin releases the instant the node voltage reaches $V_{IH}$. Setting $V(t_{release})=V_{IH}$ in (\ref{Equation1}) and solving for $t_{release}$:
\begin{equation}
        \begin{aligned}
        &V_{IH}=V_0\left(1-e^{-t_{release}/RC}\right)\\
        &\frac{V_{IH}}{V_0}=1-e^{-t_{release}/RC}\\
        &e^{-t_{release}/RC}=1-\frac{V_{IH}}{V_0}\\
        &-\frac{t_{release}}{RC}=\ln{\left(1-\frac{V_{IH}}{V_0}\right)}\\
        &t_{release}=-RC\ln{\left(1-\frac{V_{IH}}{V_0}\right)}.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{Equation4}
\end{equation}

Rearranging (\ref{Equation4}) for \textit{R}, given a target $t_{release}$ and a chosen \textit{C}:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        &R=\frac{-t_{release}}{C\ln{\left(1-\frac{V_{IH}}{V_0}\right)}}.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{Equation3}
\end{equation}

\textit{1) Worked example:} Take $V_{IH}=0.7V_0, V_0=5.0V,$ and a target $t_{release}=10\ ms$.  The $C=22\,\text{pF}$ used for the plots above was chosen only to keep the curves readable at a sub-microsecond timescale; a practical timing design instead starts from $C=100\,\text{nF}$.  Evaluating the denominator of (\ref{Equation3}):

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        &\ln{1-0.7}=\ln{0.3}\approx-1.204.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent Substituting into (\ref{Equation3}):

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        &R=\frac{-\left(10\times10^{-3}\right)}{\left(100\times10^{-9}\right)\times(-1.204)}\approx 83\,k\Omega.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

An $83\,k\Omega$ resistor with a 100\,nF capacitor gives a reset hold-off of approximately $10\,ms$.  If instead \textit{C} is kept at the plotting value of 22\,pF, the same 10\,ms target would require $R \approx 377\,\text{M}\Omega$, which is not a realistic resistor value. This is exactly why a practical design fixes a realistic capacitor value first, typically in the nanofarad range, and only then solves for the resistor.
